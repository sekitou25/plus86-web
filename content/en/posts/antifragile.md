Nassim Nicholas Taleb

These passages are translated from the excerpts saved in my Chinese notes, not quoted from the published English edition. Chapter headings are translated from those notes as well.

## Chapter 1 · Damocles' Sword and the Hydra

◆ In the end, what matters is the strength of the thread holding the sword, not the wealth and power of the person enjoying the feast.

◆ The important point is that depriving a system of stress, or of significant stressors, is not necessarily good. It can even be very harmful.

## Chapter 2 · Overcompensation and Overreaction Everywhere

◆ Most people's spare time goes to waste: it leaves them idle, lazy, and unwilling to learn. Once they become busy, they often become more active in other tasks too. Overreaction appears here again.

## Chapter 4 · What Kills Me Makes Others Stronger

◆ The unit does not grow stronger in response to stress. Instead, it dies. But a transfer of benefits takes place: other units survive, and the characteristics of those survivors improve the collective.

◆ We are talking about local errors rather than errors of the whole, small errors rather than severe and destructive ones. This helps distinguish good systems from bad ones. Good systems, such as aviation, have small errors that are independent, or even negatively correlated: one error reduces the probability of future errors. This is one way to distinguish an antifragile environment from fragile ones, such as highly internally correlated modern economic life in the era of “the world is flat.”

◆ After a mistake, those who fail often do not reflect or investigate. They feel embarrassed and cannot tolerate criticism. They try to explain away the mistake instead of enriching themselves with new information and beginning again.

◆ They are bound to care more about securing antifragility, or at least some robustness, for themselves. But that is not compatible with the collective interest or the interest of the economy as a whole. Here is the problem: the properties of the aggregate are not the properties of its parts. The whole may actually prefer its parts to be harmed.

◆ Nature wants to survive as a whole, rather than preserve every species. Each species, in turn, wants its individuals to be fragile, especially after reproduction, so the species can evolve through selection. The transfer of fragility from species to individuals is necessary for the survival of the whole.

## Chapter 5 · Open-Air Markets and Office Buildings

◆ This is a central illusion in life: believing that randomness is risky and bad, and that removing randomness will remove risk.

◆ Artificially removing randomness produces something like John's income: clear and stable, but fragile. It cannot withstand a large shock and can drop to zero at any time, though someone born in a welfare state might receive unemployment benefits. Natural randomness produces something more like George's income: it can withstand very large shocks, but it comes with daily fluctuations. Notice, too, that volatility can improve the system—which is how antifragility arises.

◆ Switzerland is like George's income. It is stable because there is variation and noise at every level. A taxi driver's daily earnings fluctuate, but annual earnings may differ little. Similarly, Switzerland remains stable overall because its cantons together form a robust system.

◆ Smallness has a certain beauty in many other respects. Small units taken together are more antifragile than a large entity. Large things are, in fact, destined to break apart; this is a mathematical property.

◆ Survival depends on the interaction between adaptability and environmental conditions.

◆ Machiavelli wrote that amid murders and civil wars, our republic grew stronger and citizens learned virtue. Small disturbances and anxieties nourish the soul. What allows a people to flourish is not peace, but freedom.

## Chapter 9 · Fat Tony and the Promoters of Fragility

◆ Curiosity is antifragile, much like an addiction: the more you satisfy it, the stronger it becomes.

## Chapter 10 · Seneca's Downside and Upside

◆ Preparing the mind for the worst can be more useful than some forms of psychotherapy, because the worst outcome of the risks I take becomes clear and definite, with harm that is limited and known. But when everything is going well, it is hard to keep up this practice of mentally reducing one's attachment—and that is precisely when it is needed most.

## Chapter 11 · Never Marry a Rock Star

◆ Antifragility combines initiative with conservative paranoia: remove the downside, protect yourself against extreme harm, and let the upside, or positive Black Swans, take its course. We have seen Seneca's asymmetry: to make the upside outweigh the downside, one need only reduce exposure to extreme harm, including emotional harm, rather than improve the middle.
