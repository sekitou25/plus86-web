A well-known, counterintuitive observation about these two emotions is that **in terms of physiological indicators alone, anxiety or fear and excitement are almost identical**.

Both are **high-arousal** emotions. They share the body's alarm system, the **sympathetic nervous system**. When either emotion arrives, the brain sounds the alarm, releasing adrenaline and cortisol and preparing the body for action.

Despite this overlap, a closer look at extreme reactions, subtle differences, and what happens afterward reveals differences in where the body places its emphasis:

## 1. Shared physical responses: sympathetic activation

Whether you are nervous about giving a presentation tomorrow or excited to ride a roller coaster, the body can produce the following core responses:

- **A faster heartbeat and higher blood pressure:** more blood is pumped to deliver oxygen to the muscles.
- **Faster, shallower breathing:** taking in more oxygen in preparation for what might happen.
- **Dilated pupils:** seeing the surroundings more clearly and taking in more visual information.
- **Slower digestion:** blood is redirected to the skeletal muscles, and digestion temporarily takes a back seat. This is why either emotion can leave your stomach feeling strange.

## 2. Subtle differences in physical experience

Although the underlying activation is similar, **how we interpret it** and the subsequent adjustments in muscles and blood vessels can produce different sensations:

### Nervousness: defense and contraction

Nervousness arises when the body perceives a **potential threat or punishment**. Its physical response leans toward **fight, flight, or freeze**:

- **Vasoconstriction and a colder skin surface:** blood vessels in the skin and extremities constrict, reducing potential blood loss in a coming “fight.” This is why nervousness can bring **cold hands and feet or a pale face**.
- **Muscle stiffness or trembling:** excessive muscle tension can cause involuntary shaking, such as trembling hands, weak knees, or an unsteady voice.
- **Digestive urgency:** severe nervousness can prompt the body to empty its load quickly, with an urgent need to use the bathroom, diarrhea, or stomach cramps—the familiar “knot in the stomach.”
- **Cold sweat:** the palms and forehead can become clammy, with eccrine sweat serving cooling and grip-related functions.

### Excitement: approach and expansion

Excitement arises when the body perceives a **potential reward or opportunity**. Its physical response leans toward an **approach system**:

- **Vasodilation and a warmer skin surface:** blood flows toward the face and torso, and metabolism speeds up. This can bring **flushed cheeks, warmth, and a little sweating**, rather than a cold sweat.
- **Energy and restlessness:** the muscles feel energized but are usually less rigid than in nervousness. There may be **more small movements**, fidgeting, pacing, or faster speech.
- **A sense of openness in the chest:** the heart is still racing, but the sensation may feel warm and outward-moving rather than tight or oppressive.

## Reframing the emotion

The original note describes more than 80% overlap between these physical responses, linking that similarity to a psychological strategy known as **anxiety reappraisal**.

When someone is extremely nervous, repeatedly saying “calm down” may not help much: the body's high-arousal state—a heart rate above 120, for example—is far from the low-arousal state associated with calm, around 70.

Saying **“I'm excited!”** instead offers another interpretation of the same signals. A racing heart and quick breathing can be read as “I'm ready to win” rather than “I'm about to mess this up.” Reinterpreting those sensations can turn defensive stress into an impulse to act.
