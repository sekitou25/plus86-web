Colm Tóibín

These passages are translated from the excerpts saved in my Chinese notes, not quoted from the published English edition.

## Chapter 1 · Lübeck, 1891

◆ “In Paraty, if you see three people, one will be talking and the other two laughing, and all of them will be wearing white.” “Are they telling jokes?” Heinrich asked. “They just laugh. That's how they are.” “But what are they laughing at?” “Darling, I don't know. But that's how they are. Sometimes I can still hear that laughter at night, coming on the wind.”

## Chapter 3 · Munich, 1893

◆ How strange, he thought, that both of them wrote fiction every day while thinking about other things, drawing strength from them and feeding their imaginations. He wondered whether his father, while signing contracts, visiting banks, and looking for investment partners, had always been thinking about private matters that made his breathing quicken.

## Chapter 4 · Munich, 1905

◆ “And me? I want two things, but they pull in opposite directions. I want fame on the stage, travel around the world, an exciting life. I also want a family and quiet years. But I can't have both. And you—you only want what you already have. You're the only one of us like that.”

## Chapter 9 · Küsnacht, 1934

◆ If Erika could be angry, he would show her what real anger was. If she could speak out, he could be more eloquent still. He did not tell Katia what he planned. He would do it alone.

◆ Readers often complained that his sentences were too long and his style too elevated. This time, he decided, his prose would soar even higher. He would address the Nazis with every system at his command. He would speak in the tones used by writers before the Nazis existed, standing above them on the ground of the German language itself. He would throw clause after clause, subordinate clauses nested within subordinate clauses, at those who inspired fear and disgust in people who believed in freedom and progress. He would ask, as though entitled to an answer, how the so-called leader had reduced Germany in just four years to a country that could no longer be accurately described. He would ask, as though no one could answer, how a writer accustomed to taking responsibility for words could remain silent while this soul-destroying regime threatened an entire continent.

## Chapter 18 · Los Angeles, 1950

◆ Although he lived in poverty and needed support, Heinrich never lost the capacity for pride. He continued to believe that his work mattered and that the causes he supported had extraordinary value.
